## Goal
One page that answers the question behind every other question: why is there a line at all, and why is it
so much longer for some countries? It explains the annual limits, how they are split between preferences,
and the per-country limit — each number cited, with a small diagram. No predictions.

## Source (already in the repository)
Section A of the Visa Bulletin itself states the limits in plain language. Use the June 2025 fixture:
```bash
node -e "import('./src/lib/extractTables.js').then(({toText})=>{const t=toText(require('fs').readFileSync('test/fixtures/official/2025-06.html','utf8'));const i=t.indexOf('STATUTORY NUMBERS');console.log(t.slice(i,i+5200))})"
```
Read the whole printed section. It gives the family and employment preference limits for the fiscal year, the
per-country limit, the dependent-area limit, each preference's share, and the rule for how numbers are allotted
(in priority-date order, and what "oversubscribed" means).
Cite it as: "Visa Bulletin for June 2025, U.S. Department of State" with the official URL from
`data/bulletins/2025/2025-06.json` (`bulletin.officialSourceUrl`). You may add the statute sections the bulletin itself
names (INA sections 201, 202, 203) as secondary sources **only if you opened the text** at `uscode.house.gov`.

## Steps
1. Add the explainer entry, `reviewedOn: null`. Title: `How the green card line works`. Body sections (`<h2>`):
   - **There is a yearly limit** — the two worldwide limits exactly as the bulletin states them for that fiscal year,
     saying "for fiscal year 2025" (limits are set per year; do not present them as permanent). Cite.
   - **The limit is split between categories** — a table: preference, the bulletin's description, the share as printed
     (percentage text copied exactly, including "plus any numbers not required by…"). Cite.
   - **No country can take more than a set share** — the per-country sentence as printed (the percentage and the number
     it works out to that year) and the dependent-area sentence. Cite.
   - **Why some countries wait longer** — only what follows from the printed rules: when demand from one country exceeds
     its limit, that country is "oversubscribed" and gets its own, earlier cut-off date; quote the bulletin's own
     definition and its list of oversubscribed areas for that month. Cite.
   - **What a cut-off date is** — the bulletin's sentence on how cut-off dates are set (visas allotted in priority-date
     order; "C" and "U" meanings as printed). Cite.
   - **What this does not tell you** — no one outside the State Department can know next month's dates; link to the
     checker and to "What happened" history (S-305) as history, not forecast.
2. `src/site/diagram.js`: `limitDiagram({ title, parts: [{ label, value }] })` → an inline SVG (`role="img"`, `<title>`
   and `<desc>` listing every label and value as text) drawing one horizontal bar split in proportion to `value`, with
   labels below. Colours `var(--series-1…5)`. Use it twice: employment preferences by percentage; family preferences by
   their printed numbers. The values passed in must be the ones you quoted in the tables (no separate typing: define
   the numbers once in the explainer and use them for both table and diagram).
3. Links: `explainerLink('how-the-line-works', 'How the line works', …)` on the home page explanation block and on the
   methodology page.
4. Tests: the diagram contains every label in its `<desc>`; segment widths sum to the bar width; the explainer validates.
5. `SHOW_DRAFTS=1 npm run dev`; read the page against the printed section, number by number. Check 375 and 1280.

## Pitfalls
- The limits change by fiscal year and the bulletin sometimes revises them mid-year. Always write the year.
- Do not explain "spillover", "recapture" or pending legislation: none of that is in the cited source.
- Percentages of the employment limit are shares of the employment level, not of all visas. Label the diagram accordingly.
